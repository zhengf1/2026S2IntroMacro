Question 1 • Data & Observation

Commodity Prices & Australia's TWI

  • Trade Weighted Index (TWI): An average of the value of the AUD against Australia's twenty largest trading partners, weighted by their trade share.
  • Two Key Questions:
    1. Why expect a positive relationship between commodity prices and the TWI?
    2. Why does the TWI exchange rate lead commodity prices?
Question 1 • Solution (Part 1)

Why the Positive Relationship?

  • • One of the major Australian exports are commodities, and commodity prices reflect the demand for these goods.
  • • An increase in the demand for commodities will be associated with an increase in demand for the AUD, as foreigners seek to import more commodities produced in Australia:
    $\text{Demand for Commodities} \uparrow \implies \text{Demand for AUD}\,(D_{\text{AUD}}) \uparrow \implies e \uparrow \text{ (AUD Appreciation)}$
Question 1 • Solution (Part 2)

Why Does the TWI Lead Commodity Prices?

  • • During periods of high global growth, there is an increased demand for resources, and this leads to higher commodity prices. Commodity prices are partly predictable based upon forecasts of world growth.
  • • If people predict that the price of a financial asset will rise in the future, then there is an incentive to purchase the asset today to achieve a capital gain.
  • • Applied to exchange rates: If people believe the AUD will become more valuable in the future due to higher predicted commodity prices, they increase demand for the AUD today to profit from future appreciation:
    $\text{Predict Global Growth} \uparrow \implies \text{Expect}\; e_{\text{future}} \uparrow \implies D_{\text{AUD}} \uparrow \text{ today} \implies e \uparrow \text{ (leads commodity prices)}$
Question 2 • Context

Greece & The Eurozone

  • • Currency Regime: Greece uses the Euro. Its currency is fixed relative to other Eurozone members, but the Euro fluctuates relative to currencies outside the Eurozone.
  • • The Debt Shock: For a prolonged period, the Greek government spent more than it raised in tax ($G > T$). Creative accounting understated government borrowing until the late 2000s before the real size of the debt and deficit was revealed.
Question 2 • Solution (Part a)

If Greece Had Its Own Currency (Drachma)

  • • Exchange Rate: The fact that the government would have a good chance of defaulting on debt would reduce the incentive for foreigners to invest in the country and lower the exchange rate (large depreciation of the Drachma).
  • • Competitiveness: Beneficial for Greek firms seeking to export (goods now relatively cheap) and Greek firms competing with foreign imports (foreign goods now relatively expensive).
  • • Real Output: This change in the exchange rate would increase net exports and help raise output:
    $e \downarrow \text{ (Depreciation)} \implies X \uparrow \text{ and } M \downarrow \implies NX \uparrow \implies Y \uparrow \text{ (Buffers Output)}$
Question 2 • Solution (Part b)

Greece Inside the Eurozone

  • • Moderate Impact on the Euro: Greece is a small member of the Eurozone, so large problems in Greece only have a moderate impact upon the value of the Euro.
  • • Limited External Stimulus: The Euro may still depreciate and stimulate exports and reduce imports, but not to the extent/size that would happen if Greece had its own currency.
  • • No Internal Competitive Advantage: It does not gain any competitive advantage relative to countries inside the Eurozone. Why? Because they have the same currency!
Question 2 • Solution (Part c)

Costs and Benefits of Joining the Euro

  • Benefits:
    Typically associated with increased trade and financial flows resulting from reduced currency risk within the Eurozone.
  • Costs:
    Domestic monetary policy has lost control, because interest rates and policy are now mostly determined in Frankfurt / Brussels (European Central Bank). Greece also lost the exchange rate as an automatic shock absorber.
